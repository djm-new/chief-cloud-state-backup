Dreyfus outlines how **China’s dominance in critical minerals** and **U.S. infrastructure bottlenecks** are creating a looming supply crisis — especially for the **commodity supercycle** driven by AI, energy transition, and national security needs.

Dreyfus warns that without domestic mining and refining capacity, the U.S. will remain **dependent on China** for the materials that power AI, EVs, and defense systems.

A counterpoint: **Can recycling and substitution offset the need for new mining?** While Dreyfus acknowledges these as partial solutions, he argues they’re not yet scalable enough to meet the **AI and green energy boom**.

**DJ should care** because this is a **capital allocation and supply chain risk** story. The **AI arms race** depends on access to these materials, and companies building large-scale AI infrastructure must consider **resource availability** and **geopolitical risk** in their long-term planning.

Now to **The Cognitive Revolution** with **Rebecca Hinds** from **Glean**, discussing **AI in the enterprise** and the surprising finding that **87% of workers use AI but only 13% report improved performance.**

Hinds introduces the concepts of **“botsitting”** and **“botshitting”** — where workers spend time managing AI outputs or correcting errors, creating **hidden labor costs** and **adoption friction.**

The strongest evidence for this is Glean’s internal research showing that **AI tools often require more oversight than expected**, especially in complex workflows. This challenges the **productivity narrative** around AI in the enterprise.

A counterpoint: **Will this change as models improve?** Hinds acknowledges that as AI becomes more reliable, the **botsitting burden may decrease**, but for now, the **productivity gains are overstated**.

**DJ should care** because this is a **reality check** on AI adoption. Enterprise software companies betting on AI to drive **efficiency gains** need to account for **user friction** and **workflow integration** — not just model capability.
