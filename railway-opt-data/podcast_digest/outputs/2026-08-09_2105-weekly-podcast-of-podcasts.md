**SAM:**  
What’s the main thesis here?

**MAYA:**  
They argue that while the AI wave has created a few trillion-dollar companies in record time—like Anthropic and OpenAI—it’s unlikely we’ll see many more in the next 3–5 years. They also discuss how fear of AI labs is making founders less ambitious, and how regulatory capture could stifle innovation.

**SAM:**  
So they’re skeptical about the next wave of trillion-dollar AI companies?

**MAYA:**  
Yes. Sarah points out that historically, it took 15–20 years for companies like Google or SpaceX to reach trillion-dollar valuations. Now, some have done it in five. But she argues that this is a rare, punctuated equilibrium—not the new normal. Most markets can’t scale revenue fast enough to support that kind of growth.

**SAM:**  
Interesting. What about the impact on founders?

**MAYA:**  
They observe that some top-tier founders are avoiding large markets out of fear of competition from well-funded AI labs like OpenAI or Anthropic. Instead, they’re going after niche opportunities with less head-to-head competition. Sarah sees this as a negative trend—founders should be more willing to compete head-on if they can offer a better product or distribution.

**SAM:**  
That’s a strong take. What’s the counterpoint?

**MAYA:**  
Elad agrees there’s a trend toward safer opportunities, especially among newer startups, but he also notes that some founders are still going head-to-head with the labs. He also emphasizes that not all markets are on the labs’ radar—there are still big opportunities to be pursued.

**SAM:**  
What about exits?

**MAYA:**  
Sarah suggests that some companies should consider selling within a 12–18 month window when valuations peak. She recommends a pre-planned, non-emotional board meeting—say, annually—to evaluate the possibility of an exit. Elad adds a new principle: ask whether the company is capturing value as capabilities become cheaper. If not, it may be time to sell.

**SAM:**  
And the regulatory angle?

**MAYA:**  
They warn about the risk of regulatory capture, drawing a parallel to the pharmaceutical industry. They cite Jensen from Jensen Pharmaceuticals, who criticized the FDA for focusing too much on safety and not enough on risk-reward balance. Sarah and Elad warn that a similar dynamic could emerge in AI labs, where safety concerns dominate to the detriment of innovation.
