Lithium, cobalt, rare earths, graphite, refining capacity — these are not just commodity inputs. MAYA: The strongest argument is that America’s industrial ambition is outrunning its materials strategy. You cannot build an AI-and-electrification future on a supply chain you do not control. The same bottlenecks show up again and again.

MAYA: The counterpoint is that supply chains can adapt. Recycling, substitution, new chemistry, friend-shoring, and demand shifts can reduce some pressure. But Dreyfus’s warning is about time. MAYA: Episode three is The Cognitive Revolution, “Babysitting the Machine,” with Rebecca Hinds from Glean. This had transcript-like page grounding, and it is the best counterweight to the grand infrastructure story. The headline is that a lot of enterprise AI usage is not yet translating into measured productivity.

SAM: The key statistic in the source material is stark: lots of workers are using AI, but only a much smaller share report meaningful performance improvement. Hinds gives language to the failure mode: “botsitting” and “botshitting.” Botsitting is the hidden labor of watching, correcting, prompting, validating, and managing AI systems. Botshitting is low-quality AI output that creates downstream cleanup work.

MAYA: That is valuable because it names a cost most AI ROI models understate. The software demo shows a clean answer. The actual enterprise workflow includes permissions, data quality, user training, hallucination checks, integration gaps, and managerial anxiety. The productivity gain can disappear if the human has to babysit the machine.

SAM: The strongest argument is that model capability is only one input into enterprise value. Workflow design may matter just as much. If the AI is outside the flow of work, or if users do not trust it, or if the organization has poor knowledge hygiene, then the model becomes another tool people have to manage. That is not automation. That is a new coordination burden.

MAYA: The counterpoint is that this may be an early-adoption problem. Interfaces will improve. Agents will get more reliable. Enterprise context windows, retrieval, permissions, and evaluation systems will mature. The first wave of SaaS also looked clunky before workflows adapted.

SAM: But the timing matters for investors and operators. If the market is pricing immediate AI-driven margin expansion, this episode says: slow down. The ROI is real only when the workflow changes. Buying AI features is not the same thing as absorbing AI into the operating system of the company.

MAYA: DJ should care because this is the difference between AI as a board-slide and AI as operating leverage. It suggests a useful diligence question: where exactly does the human stop doing work? If the answer is “the human now reviews, repairs, and coordinates the AI,” then the productivity claim is weaker than advertised.
