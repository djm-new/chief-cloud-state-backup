### **Dwarkesh Podcast – “The next big breakthrough will be AIs learning on the job”**

**MAYA:**  
Dwarkesh Patel makes a compelling case that the future of AI lies in **Reinforcement Learning with Verification and Refinement (RLVR)**. The big idea is that instead of relying solely on massive pre-training, AIs will learn in real-world environments through continuous, verifiable tasks. Labs are essentially betting that training AIs across millions of diverse RL environments will lead to AGI-like general problem-solving skills.

**SAM:**  
So this is a shift from static models to models that learn as they go?

**MAYA:**  
Exactly. The argument is that continual learning—where models update their weights during deployment—may not be necessary if in-context learning becomes powerful enough. Think of it like this: if a model can store six months of on-the-job experience in its context window, you don’t need to retrain the model. You just need longer context windows, which are improving rapidly.

**SAM:**  
But isn’t this still sample inefficient compared to humans?

**MAYA:**  
That’s the counterpoint. Right now, AIs are orders of magnitude less sample efficient than humans. But Dwarkesh argues that training is a one-time cost amortized across billions of user sessions. What matters is how smart and efficient the model is **within a session**, and that’s improving fast. He also points out that domains like coding are already seeing this—people use AIs to solve complex, long-running problems.

**SAM:**  
So why should DJ care?

**MAYA:**  
Because this reframes how we think about **product development** and **model deployment**. If AIs can learn on the job, then the future of AI isn’t just about bigger models or more data—it’s about **designing environments** where AIs can learn continuously. That has implications for **platform strategy**, **software economics**, and even how we structure **long-term autonomy** in systems.

### **a16z Podcast – “AI Is Crossing the Frontier of Human Knowledge” with Kevin Weil**

**MAYA:**  
Next, we have Kevin Weil, former VP of Science at OpenAI, on the **a16z Podcast**. His big thesis: AI is now solving problems that humans have never solved before, pushing beyond the frontier of human knowledge. He focuses on how AI is accelerating science—math, medicine, material science—and how that will shape the future of human progress.

**SAM:**  
So AI isn’t just a productivity tool—it’s a discovery engine?

**MAYA:**  
Exactly. Weil argues that the real impact of AGI will come not from chatbots or code generation, but from **scientific breakthroughs**—new materials, superconductivity, personalized medicine. He cites examples like GPT-5.2 and Gemini solving open math problems that hadn’t been solved by humans yet.

**SAM:**  
Is this really novel thinking, or just synthesis of existing knowledge?

**MAYA:**  
That’s the counterpoint. Critics say AI is just summarizing what’s already known. But Weil points out that the models are going **beyond what humans had done**—not necessarily proving they can solve problems humans couldn’t, but solving ones that hadn’t been solved yet. And he notes a pattern: AI goes from “can’t do” to “barely can do” to “great at it” in 6–12 months.

**SAM:**  
So what’s the takeaway for DJ?

**MAYA:**  
AI is becoming a **force multiplier for high-agency creators and scientists**. The frontier of AI is now in domains requiring **long-duration reasoning** and **real-world experimentation loops**. That means opportunities for startups and platforms that can support **scientific infrastructure**, **robotic labs**, and **AI-native tools** like Weil’s own *Prism*.

### **No Priors – “Why Traditional Benchmarks Fail Modern AI Models” with Noam Brown**

**MAYA:**  
Finally, on **No Priors**, Noam Brown, an OpenAI research scientist, critiques traditional AI benchmarks. He argues they’re outdated and fail to capture the true capabilities of models that can now **reason over weeks or months**.

**SAM:**  
So what’s wrong with current benchmarks?

**MAYA:**  
Most benchmarks test short-term reasoning—like answering a question in a few seconds. But modern models can now think for **hours or even days**, tackling complex, multi-step problems. Brown says we need new evaluation frameworks that reflect **long-term reasoning**, **recursive self-improvement**, and **real-world validation**.

**SAM:**  
What’s the implication for safety and evaluation?

**MAYA:**  
If models are reasoning for extended periods, we need **new safety mechanisms**. For example, how do you monitor a model that’s working on a problem for a week? Traditional red-teaming and prompt-based safety checks won’t cut it. Brown also hints at **recursive self-improvement**—models that can improve themselves over time, which has major implications for both **AI alignment** and **capability growth**.

**SAM:**  
How does this connect to the other episodes?

**MAYA:**  
It ties back to Weil’s point: AI is now doing **novel discovery**, not just summarizing. And like Dwarkesh, Brown is pushing for a shift in how we **train, evaluate, and deploy** models. This is about **long-term autonomy**, **scientific progress**, and **platform defensibility**.
